\chapter{General description of the dataset}
\noindent The subject of the project is a statistical analysis of the ``Starbucks Customer Ordering Patterns'' dataset. This dataset, which was published on \href{https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/likithagedipudi/starbucks-customer-ordering-patterns}{Kaggle}\footnote{The dataset is named Starbucks Customer Ordering Patterns and was sourced from: https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/likithagedipudi/starbucks-customer-ordering-patterns}, consists of 100 000 real transactions made by Starbucks customers over a two-year period (2024-2025). It allows to analyse customer behavior, focusing on how orders are placed, the financial value of those orders, and the time required for fulfillment. To ensure the methods are adjusted to the dataset, five key features have been isolated to analyze trends in spending, demographic preferences, and operational efficiency.

\section{Attributes of the dataset}
Below there is a list of all attributes of the dataset.
\begin{itemize}
    \item IDs:
          \begin{itemize}
              \item customer \_id: unique identifier for each customer (15 000 unique profiles),
              \item order\_id: unique transaction id (100 000 orders),
              \item store\_id: identifier for each store.
          \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Date:
          \begin{itemize}
              \item order\_date: (ranging from 01.2024 to 12.2025),
              \item order\_time,
              \item day\_of\_week.
          \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Order:
          \begin{itemize}
              \item order\_channel: method used to place the order (Mobile App, Drive-Thru, In-Store Cashier or Kiosk),
              \item store\_location\_type: (Urban, Suburban or Rural)
              \item region: (Northeast, Southeast, Midwest, West or Southwest),
              \item customer\_age\_group: (18-24, 25-34, 35-44, 45-54 or 55+),
              \item customer\_gender: (Male, Female or Other),
              \item is\_rewards\_member: whether the customer is a member of the Starbucks Rewards program (True or False),
              \item cart\_size: number of items in the order,
              \item num\_customizations: number of modifications made to the order (extra shots, syrup, different milk),
              \item total\_spend: total cost of the transaction in USD\@,
              \item fulfillment\_time\_min: time (in minutes) from the moment the order is placed until it is ready for the customer,
              \item drink\_category: (Coffee, Tea, Refresher or Other),
              \item has\_food\_item: (True or False),
              \item order\_ahead: whether the order was placed ahead of time (True or False),
              \item customer\_satisfaction: rating from 1 to 5 (correlated with fulfillment time and channel).

          \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Testing of the hypothesis about the normality of distributions}
\section{Formulation of the Hypotheses}
To determine the appropriate statistical methods for further analysis, it is necessary to test whether the continuous variables in the dataset specifically the total cost of the transaction ( \texttt{total\_spend} ) and the time from order placement to completion ( \texttt{fulfillment\_time\_min} ) — follow a normal (Gaussian) distribution.
For each of these variables, the following statistical hypotheses are established:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\mathbf{H_0}$:The distribution of the analyzed variable follows a normal distribution.
    \item $\mathbf{H_1}$:The distribution of the analyzed variable does not follow a normal distribution.

\end{itemize}
The adopted level of significance for all statistical tests in this report is alpha=0.05.
\section{Visual Assessment Using Q-Q Plots}
To initially assess the distribution of the continuous variables, Quantile-Quantile (Q-Q) plots were generated. A Q-Q plot compares the quantiles of the sample data against the theoretical quantiles of a perfectly normal distribution. If the observed data is normally distributed, the data points will align closely with the diagonal reference line.

\section{Formal Hypothesis Testing (Kolmogorov-Smirnov)}
To conclusively determine whether the data follows a normal distribution, a Kolmogorov-Smirnov (K-S) test was conducted. The K-S test operates by comparing the actual distribution of the dataset against a theoretically perfect normal curve. In this test, the null hypothesis assumes that the data is perfectly normal. The resulting p-value indicates the probability of seeing the current data if it were truly normal; a p-value below the standard threshold of 0.05 means we must reject the null hypothesis and conclude that the data significantly deviates from a normal distribution.
\subsection{total\_spend}
In Table \ref{tab:ks_total_spend} we can see the Skewness and Kurtosis results for total amount spent.
\begin{table}[H]

    \centering

    \end{tabular}

    \caption{Kolmogorov-Smirnov test results for total\_spend}
    \label{tab:ks_total_spend}
\end{table}
For the \texttt{total\_spend variable}, the K-S test returned p-value equal $2.2\cdot10^{-16}$
Because this p-value is vastly smaller than 0.05, we decisively reject the null hypothesis $H_0$, confirming that the distribution of total spend is not normal.

\section{Conclusion on Normality}
Based on visual, descriptive, and formal mathematical testing, we conclude that the distribution of both \texttt{total\_spend} and \texttt{fulfillment\_time\_min} deviates from strict normality.
For \texttt{total\_spend}, this deviation is driven by a small number of high-value purchases that stretch the data upward. However, eliminating these outliers still results in a distribution that deviates from normality.
For \texttt{fulfillment\_time\_min}, the data is highly symmetrical with only 0.81\% outliers but is cleanly truncated by a 1-minute physical minimum required to fulfill an order. Ultimately, these results perfectly reflect the real-world operational realities of the business: transaction values have no ceiling, and fulfillment times have a hard physical floor.

This dataset contains operational metrics from multiple restaurants distributed across various locations. To evaluate the data, we calculated the mean fulfillment time for each individual restaurant.     \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\section{Analysis of customization patterns across age groups}
Firstly, we wanted to investigate whether a relationship exists between a customer's age group and their tendency to customize orders.
\subsection{Formulation of the Hypothesis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item $H_0$: The distribution of the number of customizations is identical across all customer age groups.
    \item $H_1$: At least one customer age group differs significantly from the others in their number of customizations.
\end{itemize}
\subsection{Methodology}
To determine if drink customization habits vary by demographic, we analyzed the relationship between the \texttt{customer\_age\_group} and \texttt{num\_customizations} attributes. Because the number of customizations is a discrete numerical variable with a heavily right-skewed distribution, we utilized the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis H test. A standard significance level of $\alpha = 0.05$ was applied.
\subsection{Results}
A Kruskal-Wallis H test was conducted to determine if there were differences in the number of drink customizations between five customer age groups: 18-24, 25-34, 35-44, 45-54, and 55+. The test revealed a statistically significant difference in customization patterns across the age demographics ($\chi^2 = 1338.6$, $p < 0.001$). Therefore, we reject the null hypothesis ($H_0$).

There was no statistically significant difference in the number of customizations between the 18-24 and 25-34 age groups ($p = 0.84$), nor between the 35-44 and 45-54 age groups ($p = 0.41$). However, differences between all other age group combinations were highly significant ($p < 0.001$). The visual representation of these differences is provided in Figure \ref{fig:age_customizations}. The boxplot highlights a clear generational divide: while younger customers (18-34) frequently request multiple customizations, the median number of modifications decreases significantly for groups aged 35 and above. \section{Analysis of customer satisfaction across order channels}
Secondly, we wanted to investigate whether a relationship exists between the customer's chosen ordering method and their satisfaction ratings. This analysis is crucial to evaluate the effectiveness of digital channels in the Starbucks ecosystem.

\subsection{Formulation of the Hypothesis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\mathbf{H_0}$: The distribution of customer satisfaction ratings is identical across all ordering channels.
    \item $\mathbf{H_1}$: At least one ordering channel differs significantly from the others in terms of satisfaction ratings.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Methodology}
To determine if satisfaction levels vary by ordering method, we analyzed the relationship between the \texttt{order\_channel} and \texttt{customer\_satisfaction} attributes. Because satisfaction is measured on a discrete ordinal scale (1-5) and the dataset exhibits non-normal distributions, we utilized the non-parametric \textbf{Kruskal-Wallis H test}. A standard significance level of $\alpha = 0.05$ was applied.

\subsection{Results}
The Kruskal-Wallis test revealed a statistically significant difference in satisfaction ratings across the different channels ($\chi^2 = 2073.6$, $p < 0.001$). Therefore, we reject the null hypothesis ($\mathbf{H_0}$).

\section{Do Rewards Members Spend More Than Non-Members?}
Starbucks offers a rewards program to incentivize customer loyalty. In this section, we analyze whether enrollment in this program correlates with higher transaction values. Given that the \texttt{total\_spend} variable was previously found to be non-normally distributed, we utilize the median as a robust measure of central tendency and the Mann-Whitney U test for hypothesis testing.

\begin{table}[h]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{|l|l|}
        \hline
        \multicolumn{1}{|c|}{\textbf{Group}} & \textbf{Median Spend (USD)} \\
        \hline
        Rewards Members                      & 15.12                       \\
        Non-Rewards Members                  & 13.40                       \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Comparison of median spending between customer groups.}
    \label{tab:median_spend}
\end{table}

\subsection{Formulation of the Hypothesis}
We test the following hypotheses at a significance level of $\alpha = 0.05$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $H_0$: The distribution of total spend is the same for both groups.
    \item $H_1$: The distribution of total spend differs significantly between the two groups.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Methodology and Results}
To evaluate the statistical significance of the \$1.72 difference in medians, a Mann-Whitney U test was performed. This non-parametric test is appropriate given the skewness of the spending 3data.

The test yielded the following results:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Test Statistic ($W$):} 1033353000
    \item \textbf{$p$-value:} $< 2.2e-16$
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Interpretation}
Since the $p$-value is significantly lower than $\alpha = 0.05$, we reject the null hypothesis $H_0$. This provides strong evidence that the difference in spending patterns is statistically significant. The results confirm that members of the rewards program tend to have higher transaction values than non-members.


\section{Impact of Drink Customization on Fulfillment Time}
To further explore operational efficiency, we analyzed whether the complexity of an order---measured by the number of customizations---affects the time required to fulfill it. Following the findings in Section 4.1, we focused this analysis on two specific categories: \texttt{Single Espresso} and \texttt{Single Brewed Coffee}.

\subsection{Formulation of the Hypothesis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\mathbf{H_0}$: There is no correlation between the number of customizations and fulfillment time.
    \item $\mathbf{H_1}$: There is a significant correlation between the number of customizations and fulfillment time.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Methodology}
The relationship was investigated using the \textbf{Spearman's Rank Correlation Coefficient}, which is appropriate for non-normally distributed data and discrete variables. We visualized the relationship by plotting the mean fulfillment time for each customization level with standard error bars to account for variability.

\subsection{Results and Interpretation}
As illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:customization_fulfillment_averages}, the mean fulfillment time remains remarkably stable across different levels of customization for both beverage types.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{../Resources/Hypothesis_testing/Customization_vs_Fulfillment_Averages.pdf}
    \caption{Mean fulfillment time vs. number of customizations for Espresso and Brewed Coffee}
    \label{fig:customization_fulfillment_averages}
\end{figure}

The statistical analysis confirmed this visual observation:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Single Espresso}: $\rho = 0.023$, $p = 0.497$
    \item \textbf{Single Brewed Coffee}: $\rho = -0.041$, $p = 0.233$
\end{itemize}

In both cases, the $p$-values are significantly higher than the threshold of 0.05, meaning we \textbf{fail to reject the null hypothesis}. This suggests that for these standardized beverage types, the number of customizations (such as extra shots or syrup) does not significantly impede the fulfillment process. This reflects high operational efficiency and standardization in the drink preparation workflow.

\section{The correlation between Satisfaction and Fulfillment Time}

\subsection{Formulation of the Hypothesis}
Following the preliminary claim from Section 1.1 that customer satisfaction correlates with fulfillment time, we performed a statistical test to evaluate the strength of this relationship. We establish the following hypotheses at a significance level of $\alpha = 0.05$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $H_0$: There is no monotonic relationship between fulfillment time and customer satisfaction ($\rho = 0$).
    \item $H_1$: There is a statistically significant monotonic relationship between fulfillment time and customer satisfaction ($\rho \neq 0$).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Methodology}
Given that customer satisfaction is serial data (measured on a discrete 1 to 5 scale), a Spearman's rank correlation test was utilized. Fulfillment time was measured continuously in minutes.

\subsection{Results and Interpretation}
To visualize the relationship, fulfillment times were categorized into 2-minute bins, and the average satisfaction was plotted for each bin (Figure \ref{fig:satisfaction_vs_fulfillment}).

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{../Resources/Hypothesis_testing/satisfaction_vs_fulfillment.pdf}
    \caption{Average customer satisfaction across different fulfillment time bins.}
    \label{fig:satisfaction_vs_fulfillment}
\end{figure}

The statistical test yielded the following results:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Spearman's $\rho$ Estimate:} \textit{-0.03311538}
    \item \textbf{$p$-value:} \textit{2.2e-16}
\end{itemize}


Since the $p$-value is significantly lower than $\alpha = 0.05$, we reject the null hypothesis $H_0$. The test confirms a statistically significant negative correlation between fulfillment time and customer satisfaction. The downward trend displayed in Figure \ref{fig:satisfaction_vs_fulfillment} visually supports this, illustrating that as customer wait times increase, average satisfaction scores steadily decline. Consequently, operational improvements focused on minimizing wait times should be heavily prioritized to maintain high customer satisfaction.


\chapter{Multiple Linear Regression: Predicting Fulfillment Time}
This chapter transitions from basic hypothesis testing to predictive statistical modeling by applying Multiple Linear Regression to the dataset.
We wanted to see how different variables $($specifically order complexity and ordering channels$)$ interact to impact overall order fulfillment time.
\section{Introduction}
To introduce an advanced statistical modeling technique, a multiple linear regression model was developed to predict the time required to complete an order (\texttt{fulfillment\_time\_min}). The independent variables selected for this model include the number of customizations (\texttt{num\_customizations}), the total number of items in the order (\texttt{cart\_size}), and the ordering method (\texttt{order\_channel}).

\section{Methodology}
The theoretical model is expressed by the following equation:
$$ FT = \beta_0 + \beta_1(NC) + \beta_2(CS) + \sum \beta_k(OC_k) + \epsilon $$
Where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $FT$ = \texttt{fulfillment\_time\_min}
    \item $NC$ = \texttt{num\_customizations}
    \item $CS$ = \texttt{cart\_size}
    \item $OC$ = \texttt{order\_channel}
\end{itemize}
Since \texttt{order\_channel} is a categorical variable, it was encoded as dummy variables within the model. The standard assumptions of linear regression were verified using diagnostic residual plots.
\section{Results and Interpretation}
The multiple linear regression model yielded an Adjusted R-squared value of 0.3587, indicating that approximately 35.87\% of the variance in fulfillment time can be explained by these predictors. The overall model was statistically significant ($p < 0.001$).

Looking at the individual coefficients, the results revealed a surprising operational insight. Neither the number of customizations ($p = 0.495$) nor the cart size ($p = 0.524$) were statistically significant predictors of fulfillment time. The coefficient estimates for both variables were practically zero ($-0.002$ and $-0.001$, respectively). This suggests that order complexity does not create meaningful operational bottlenecks.

Instead, the ordering method (\texttt{order\_channel}) is the primary driver of fulfillment time. The baseline expected wait time (representing the Drive-Thru channel, captured by the intercept) is 5.81 minutes. In contrast, all other channels are significantly faster ($p < 0.001$): placing an order via the In-Store Cashier reduces the expected wait time by 2.60 minutes, using a Kiosk reduces it by 1.79 minutes, and using the Mobile App reduces it by 1.29 minutes compared to the Drive-Thru.

\chapter{Results and conclusions}
\section{Executive Summary of Empirical Findings}
The comprehensive statistical evaluation of the Starbucks dataset provides clear evidence regarding customer behaviors, operational efficiency, and the impact of digital channels. By analyzing over 100,000 transactions, we identified key drivers of both revenue and customer satisfaction.

\section{Strategic Business Insights}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Digital Migration and Loyalty Programs:} Our analysis proved that the Mobile App channel achieves the highest customer satisfaction score (3.86) compared to traditional channels like the Drive-Thru (3.44). Furthermore, the Mann-Whitney U test confirmed that Starbucks Rewards members spend significantly more (median \$15.12) than non-members (\$13.40). \\
    \textit{Strategic Action:} Starbucks should aggressively push digital adoption and loyalty program enrollment, as these are proven drivers of both higher revenue and an improved customer experience.

   \item \textbf{Monetizing Customization Without Operational Drag:} A strong generational trend exists, with younger demographics (18–34) utilizing customizations heavily. Crucially, our Spearman's rank correlation analysis revealed that for core beverages (like Espresso and Brewed Coffee), these customizations \textbf{do not} significantly impede fulfillment time. \\
    \textit{Strategic Action:} Marketing teams can safely promote high-margin customizations to younger audiences without the fear of creating operational bottlenecks or slowing down service at the bar.

    \newpage

    \item \textbf{Speed as a Direct Driver of Satisfaction:} A statistically significant negative correlation was found between fulfillment time and customer satisfaction. While average store times converge toward normality, any individual delays directly harm the customer experience. \\
    \textit{Strategic Action:} Operational improvements must ruthlessly focus on minimizing wait times. Addressing the higher variance observed in brewed coffee fulfillment (compared to standardized espresso production) could yield immediate improvements in overall satisfaction ratings.
\end{itemize}
